    Reading the tide

    Three ideas explain most of what you will see on the shore. The toggle under the scene lets you try the second one.

    Why twice a day

    The moon's gravity pulls the ocean into a bulge on the side facing it and another on the far side. As Earth turns, most coastlines pass through both bulges each day, so high tide comes around every 12 hours and 25 minutes.

    Spring and neap

    Near new and full moon, the sun and moon pull in line and the tides swing widest. These are spring tides, named for the water springing up, not the season. Near the half moons the pulls partly cancel and the range shrinks. Those are neap tides.

    When to go

    Arrive about an hour before low tide and head back as the water turns. The lowest low tides come on spring tides, which makes those the best days to see the lower shore.

    Visiting the shore

    A rocky shore is a living place that also happens to be walkable. A few habits keep it that way.

    • Walk on bare rock and sand.Mussels and barnacles crunch underfoot, and they take years to recover.
    • Put rocks back where they were.Animals live underneath. Lift a rock gently, look, then set it down the same way up.
    • Look, don't collect.A shell or sea star taken home is a resident removed. A photo lasts longer.
    • Watch the water, not just the pools.Check a local tide table before you go, and keep an eye on the sea as the tide turns.
    • Wear shoes with grip.Wet rock and seaweed are slippery. Boots or old sneakers beat sandals.
    • Keep your hands gentle.If you touch an animal use a wet finger, and wash off sunscreen first.

    Tideline is an invented field guide. The tide model on this page is simplified, so always check a local tide table before you visit the shore.
